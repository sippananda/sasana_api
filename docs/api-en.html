How is Malaysia's API calculated?

The lag of the 24-hour average, and a comparison with Singapore, the United States, China and Hong Kong

A parent with AI assistant · 18 October 2026

Abstract

Malaysia's Air Pollutant Index (API) describes the average air quality over the preceding 24 hours rather than the air at the present moment. As a result, the index rises slowly when haze arrives and falls equally slowly after the haze has cleared. This article explains how the API is calculated, examines the delay introduced by the 24-hour average using hourly readings from four stations near the school, compares the Malaysian approach with those of Singapore, the United States, China and Hong Kong, and discusses two ways of obtaining more immediate PM2.5 data.

Keywords: Air Pollutant Index; PM2.5; rolling average; lag; haze

This article is a personal compilation, provided for information only. The author writes as an individual parent. It does not represent the position of the school and does not constitute health advice.

1 Introduction

Many parents will recognise the following situation. The sky is grey and the air smells of smoke, yet the official API still reads "Moderate". At the same moment, a foreign air-quality app may well report "Unhealthy". The question is which of the two numbers is correct.

In many cases both are. The two numbers answer different questions. This article addresses three points:

  • how the API is calculated;
  • why a 24-hour average lags behind the actual air quality;
  • how Singapore, the United States, China and Hong Kong address the same problem.

2 What the API is

The API is published by the Department of Environment (DOE). It expresses the level of air pollution as a single number without a unit; a higher number indicates poorer air quality.

Table 1.　API categories and DOE health advice

API Status DOE health advice (summarised)
0–50 Good No restriction on outdoor activities
51–100 Moderate No restriction on outdoor activities
101–200 Unhealthy Elderly people, pregnant women, children and people with heart or lung conditions should reduce outdoor activities
201–300 Very unhealthy High-risk groups should remain indoors
Above 300 Hazardous The public should avoid outdoor activities

The DOE operates 68 monitoring stations nationwide, each of which reports its API every hour. The official sources are the APIMS website and the MyJAS EQMS mobile application.

3 How the API is calculated

The API of a station is the highest of six sub-indexes, one for each pollutant. The DOE's calculation document describes the procedure in three steps [1]:

  1. Six pollutants are measured: PM2.5, PM10, ozone, carbon monoxide, nitrogen dioxide and sulphur dioxide.
  2. Each pollutant is averaged over its own period. The periods differ because acceptable exposure times differ between pollutants.
  3. Each average is converted into a sub-index between 0 and 500, and the highest sub-index is taken. This value is reported as the API of the station.

Table 2.　Averaging period of each pollutant

Pollutant Averaging period
PM2.5 (fine particles) 24 hours
PM10 (coarser particles) 24 hours
Ozone 8 hours, and also 1 hour
Carbon monoxide 8 hours
Nitrogen dioxide 1 hour
Sulphur dioxide 1 hour

For example, if a station has a PM2.5 sub-index of 152, an ozone sub-index of 40 and lower values for the remaining pollutants, its API is 152 and PM2.5 is the dominant pollutant.

According to the DOE, particulate matter determines the API most of the time in Malaysia, and almost always during haze episodes. The DOE document gives 2017 as the year in which PM2.5 was added to the calculation, whereas news reports give August 2018 as the start of public reporting. The conversion formula is given in Appendix A.

4 What is published and what is not

The DOE publishes the hourly API of each station and the formula used to calculate it, but not the hourly PM2.5 concentrations.

  • Published: the API of every station for every hour, together with its status.
  • Published: the formula for converting PM2.5 and the other pollutants into the API, in an official "API Calculation" document in Malay and English.
  • Not published: the hourly PM2.5 concentration in micrograms per cubic metre. Assoc. Prof. Helena Varkkey of Universiti Malaya has noted that the government holds these data but does not release them [4].

The public therefore sees only the smoothed 24-hour figure, and not the hourly readings that would show the current state of the air.

A caveat: the DOE has issued two official versions of its calculation document: a September 2021 version on the DOE website, and a version published on 14 October 2026 on the APIMS website. Their PM2.5 values differ [1, 2] (see Appendix A). This article follows the newer October 2026 version. The difference does not affect the main conclusion, namely that the 24-hour average delays the API.

5 Why a 24-hour average lags behind

A 24-hour rolling average replaces only one twenty-fourth of its data each hour. It therefore takes about a day to reflect the full effect of new haze, and about a day to recover once the haze has cleared.

  • Each hour, the newest reading is added and the reading from 24 hours earlier is removed.
  • Because the newest hour accounts for only 1/24 of the total, the API reaches its peak later than the actual air quality does, and the peak is lower.
  • A study of data from Bangkok found that this type of average lags behind the hourly readings by about 10 to 13 hours [11].

When haze arrives, the US NowCast responds within a few hours. The 24-hour average, by contrast, reaches its maximum only when the haze clears, and takes a further full day to return to its earlier level.

5.1 An example from the school monitor

  • At 2:00 pm on 17 October, the average API of the four stations was 150. This figure represents the air from 3:00 pm on 16 October to 2:00 pm on 17 October, not only the air at 2:00 pm.
  • At 10:00 am on 18 October, the average was still 139, only 11 points below the previous afternoon. Even when the air improves, the index declines slowly.
  • An exception: under the October 2026 conversion table, the entire range from API 101 to 150 corresponds to only about 5 µg/m³ of PM2.5, so the API can change very quickly within this range. Between 4:00 pm and 8:00 pm on 16 October, the Kuala Selangor station fell from 152 to 99. Near 150, a very small change in concentration is enough to move the API across that value.

5.2 Hourly readings at the four stations

The figure below shows all hourly API readings at the four stations from the evening of 8 October to 11:00 am on 18 October [12]. The light blue band marks the range from API 100 to 150.

  • Individual stations pass through the 100–150 range quickly, usually within a few hours. Examples are the decline on 10–11 October, the rise on 13–14 October and the sharp fall at Kuala Selangor on the evening of 16 October. This is consistent with the steepness of this part of the conversion table.
  • The four-station average, in contrast, can remain within this range for much longer. Since 17 October, the three Kuala Lumpur and Selangor stations have remained above 150, while Kuala Selangor has remained below 100; the average therefore falls in between.
  • An average between 100 and 150 therefore does not imply that any single station lies in this range. More often, it indicates a large difference between stations.
  • Kuala Selangor frequently behaves differently. The station is located on the coast, generally reads lower than the other three and rises and falls more sharply. Sea breezes may explain this, although this has not yet been verified against wind data.

6 Approaches in other countries

Singapore, the United States, China and Hong Kong also use averages of 24 hours or longer to assess health risk, but each additionally provides the public with a more immediate figure. Of the five places compared here, Malaysia is the only one that provides the 24-hour figure alone.

Table 3.　Comparison of air-quality indices in five places

Place Main index Averaging period of the main index More immediate figure Official guidance on use
Malaysia API PM2.5: 24-hour rolling average None Use the API
Singapore [6] PSI 24 hours 1-hour PM2.5 reading in four bands (Normal, Elevated, High, Very High); banded since 2016 1-hour PM2.5 for immediate activities; 24-hour PSI forecast for planning the next day
United States [7] AQI Daily AQI: 24-hour average, midnight to midnight NowCast: a weighted average of the last 12 hours, close to a 3-hour average when air quality changes rapidly The "current" value on official maps and apps is the NowCast
China [8] AQI Daily report: 24-hour average Hourly report with both the 1-hour PM2.5 concentration and the 24-hour average Daily report for the day as a whole; hourly report for current conditions
Hong Kong [9] AQHI (1 to 10+) 3-hour moving average; the health risks of four pollutants are added together The index itself is based on 3 hours; hourly PM2.5 concentrations also published since 2012 Adjust activities according to the health-risk category

Several points emerge from this comparison:

  • Singapore follows the approach closest to Malaysia's, but also publishes a 1-hour PM2.5 reading. As the two countries are often compared, it is important first to establish whether a given figure is a 1-hour or a 24-hour value.
  • The United States NowCast is a compromise: in stable conditions it behaves like a 12-hour average, and when air quality changes rapidly it behaves like a 3-hour average. In an example given by the US Environmental Protection Agency, the NowCast halves with each hour after a one-hour peak, whereas the 24-hour average for that day retains only one twenty-fourth of the peak.
  • Hong Kong adopted the AQHI at the end of 2013. Because it uses a 3-hour average, it responds most quickly of the five.

In 2023, the Malaysian researchers Varkkey and Ashfold argued that the DOE could publish hourly PM2.5 data without new equipment, as this would only require the release of data that already exist [4].

7 Discussion

  • The two kinds of figure answer different questions. The 24-hour average reflects cumulative exposure over the past day and can be compared with health standards defined over 24 hours; the hourly figure reflects the air at the present moment.
  • The difference between them is greatest when air quality changes rapidly. When haze first arrives, the 24-hour average understates current conditions; just after the haze clears, it overstates them (see Figure 1). In stable conditions the two are similar.
  • Official practice elsewhere. Singapore's National Environment Agency advises using the 1-hour PM2.5 reading for immediate activities and the 24-hour PSI forecast for planning the next day. Official maps in the United States show the NowCast as the current value.
  • Academic views. Dr Fatimah Ahamad of Sunway University told SAYS that a longer averaging period reflects cumulative health risk, but may mask a haze episode that is still developing [4].
  • Figures from foreign apps cannot be compared directly with the API. In October 2026 the DOE explained that the scales, averaging periods and update intervals differ [5].
  • The sensitivity differs between parts of the conversion table. Under the October 2026 version, the band from API 101 to 150 is very steep: a change of only about 5 µg/m³ of PM2.5 moves the API between 100 and 150. This applies to rises and falls alike, so within this band small changes appear large. The band from API 151 to 200 is flat: when PM2.5 rises from 60 to 110 µg/m³, almost doubling, the API rises only from about 153 to 179, so changes during severe haze appear small. On the evening of 16 October, for example, the Kuala Selangor station fell from 152 to 99 within four hours, while the PM2.5 derived from the table fell only from about 57 to about 50 µg/m³.
  • Compared with the older version, the new one reaches the thresholds earlier. For PM2.5 between about 50 and 150 µg/m³, the October 2026 version gives a higher API than the September 2021 version. Under the new version, the school threshold of 150 corresponds to a 24-hour average PM2.5 of about 55 µg/m³ and the threshold of 180 to about 112 µg/m³; under the older version the figures are about 113 and 135.
  • Comparison with health standards. The World Health Organization does not publish an index like the API; it sets guideline values for PM2.5 concentration: 15 µg/m³ for the 24-hour average and 5 µg/m³ for the annual average, together with four interim targets [13]. Malaysia's 2020 ambient air quality standard for the 24-hour average is 35 µg/m³ [14]. Under the October 2026 version, API 100, the upper limit of "Moderate", corresponds to 50.4 µg/m³, which is above both Malaysia's own 2020 standard and the WHO guideline value (Table 4).

Table 4.　Reference values for 24-hour average PM2.5 (µg/m³)

Reference value 24-hour average PM2.5
WHO guideline value (2021) 15
WHO interim target 4 25
Malaysian ambient air quality standard (2020) 35
WHO interim target 3 37.5
WHO interim target 2; Malaysian interim target (2018) 50

8 Sources of real-time PM2.5

The API lags behind the actual air, and the DOE does not publish hourly PM2.5 concentrations. Two routes are available for obtaining a more immediate picture.

8.1 A nearby low-cost sensor

  • Since 18 October 2026, this website has recorded the hourly readings of a low-cost sensor in Rawang Anggun (Jalan Anggun 3P). The sensor is about 2.7 km from the school, whereas the nearest DOE station is 19 km away.
  • The sensor is a GAIA A08 with a single Plantower PMS5003 laser particle counter; its data are published through aqicn.org [10].
  • The website compares its readings with the four DOE stations on the sensor comparison page. So far there are too few data to draw conclusions.
  • This source has several limitations:
    • The sensor is privately owned and may go offline or be moved at any time.
    • aqicn reports a US AQI value rather than a concentration, which has to be converted.
    • Low-cost sensors tend to read high in humid air and need to be calibrated against DOE data.
    • With a single counter, a fault is difficult to detect.

8.2 A sensor at the school

The school could also consider installing its own sensor on the campus. It would then measure the air at the school itself, and the data would be under the school's control.

Table 5.　Examples of low-cost sensors suitable for a school

Model Price (seen in October 2026) Particle sensor Data
GAIA A08 EUR 49 1 × PMS5003 Uploaded to the aqicn map automatically
AirGradient Open Air CAD 315 (Canadian retailer) PMS5003T Open source; can send data to one's own server
PurpleAir Not checked 2 × Plantower, which cross-check each other PurpleAir map; the most widely field-tested
  • Such sensors need Wi-Fi and an outdoor USB power supply, and should be placed under cover but in free-flowing air.
  • Two sensors are more reliable than one: if one fails the other continues, and a disagreement between them reveals a problem early.
  • A new sensor is best run for one to two weeks alongside the DOE 24-hour averages to establish a correction factor.
  • On this website, sensor data are shown for information only; the school rule uses the official DOE API alone.

9 Limitations

  • The data cover only 8 to 18 October 2026, about ten days, at four monitoring stations.
  • The DOE publishes only the API and not hourly PM2.5 concentrations. All concentrations mentioned in this article are derived from the API using the conversion table.
  • The DOE has not stated from which date the new conversion table has been used to calculate the API. The data checks in this article show that the readings from 10 to 18 October fit the October 2026 version but not the September 2021 version, which suggests that the new table was in use before the document was published.
  • The link between the Kuala Selangor readings and sea breezes is a conjecture that has not been verified against wind data.
  • The author is not an air-quality specialist. In case of any discrepancy, the DOE's information takes precedence.

10 Conclusion

  • The API is the highest of six sub-indexes; during haze it is usually determined by PM2.5.
  • PM2.5 is averaged over a rolling 24-hour period, so the API lags the actual air quality by roughly half a day and its peaks are reduced.
  • The DOE publishes the conversion formula but not the hourly PM2.5 concentrations.
  • Singapore, the United States, China and Hong Kong all provide the public with a more immediate figure. If Malaysia also published hourly PM2.5 data, parents would be able to make better-timed decisions.

Appendix A. Conversion of PM2.5 to a sub-index

Each sub-index is calculated with the same piecewise linear formula: the band in which the concentration lies is identified first, and the concentration is then scaled within that band.

SI = (Ihigh − Ilow) ÷ (Chigh − Clow) × (C − Clow) + Ilow

  • C: the 24-hour average PM2.5 concentration (µg/m³)
  • C_low, C_high: the lower and upper concentration limits of the band
  • I_low, I_high: the lower and upper index values of the band

The DOE has issued two official documents: a September 2021 version on the DOE website and a version dated 14 October 2026 on the APIMS website. They differ in the PM2.5 bands between API 100 and 200 [1, 2], which is precisely the range most often observed during haze:

Table 6.　PM2.5 bands in the two DOE conversion tables (µg/m³)

API band October 2026 version (µg/m³) September 2021 version (µg/m³)
0–50 0–12.0 0–12.0
51–100 12.1–50.4 12.1–75.5
101–150 50.5–55.4 75.5–150.4 (a single band for 101–200)
151–200 55.5–150.4 as above
201–300 150.5–250.4 150.5–250.4
301–400 250.5–350.4 250.5–350.4
401–500 350.5–500.4 350.5–500.4

The slope is the number of API points added for each additional µg/m³ of PM2.5. The October 2026 version is steepest between API 101 and 150 (slope 10) and flattest between 151 and 200 (slope 0.52), whereas the September 2021 version has a single band above API 100 with a slope of 1.32.

As an example, consider a 24-hour average PM2.5 concentration of 100 µg/m³:

  • October 2026 version: SI = 49 ÷ 94.9 × (100 − 55.5) + 151 ≈ 174
  • September 2021 version: SI = 99 ÷ 74.9 × (100 − 75.5) + 101 ≈ 133

The same concentration therefore yields two results that differ by about 40 points.

Appendix B. Glossary

  • PM2.5: particles with a diameter of 2.5 micrometres or less. They penetrate deep into the lungs and form the main component of haze.
  • Sub-index: the index derived from the average concentration of a single pollutant.
  • Rolling average: an average recalculated every hour, always over the most recent 24 hours.
  • NowCast: the US estimate of current air quality, which gives greater weight to the most recent hours.
  • Lag: the delay between a change in the actual air quality and the corresponding change in the index.
